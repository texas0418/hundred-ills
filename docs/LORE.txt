HUNDRED ILLS - LORE AND SOURCING
Compiled 2026-08-19.

READ THIS FIRST
Every entry is marked with how solid it is. This matters more than usual,
because the entire product claim is "this is real and you can look it up."
If we ship something invented while implying it is attested, the whole
thing collapses into the pastiche we are trying to avoid.

  [A] Well attested. Named in Ming/Qing texts or solid modern scholarship.
  [B] Widely held folk belief, regionally variable, thinly documented.
  [C] My extrapolation or design invention. NOT real. Never implied real.

Anything the almanac app presents as a real entry must be [A] or [B],
and [B] entries must say "in some places" or equivalent. [C] material
lives only in the fiction, never in the reference layer.

BEFORE BUILD: every [A] below needs a primary or scholarly citation
attached. I have written them from knowledge, not from sources open in
front of me. Treat this file as a research plan, not a bibliography.


=============================================================
THE FRAME RITUAL
=============================================================

走百病  zou bai bing  "walking off the hundred illnesses"   [A]
Also 散百病, 游百病, 走百病儿 depending on region.

Ming and Qing. Night of the fifteenth or sixteenth of the first lunar
month - the sixteenth in many northern and Sichuan/Guizhou accounts, the
fifteenth elsewhere, folded into Lantern Festival. Women went out after
dark, in groups, and walked. The walking itself shed the coming year's
sickness.

Components, all [A] but not all present in every account:

  走三桥   crossing three bridges. Jiangnan variant especially. Some
           accounts require three DIFFERENT bridges, never doubling back.
           THIS IS THE MAP. See DECISIONS 14.

  摸钉     mo ding. Touching the bronze studs on a city gate. A pun:
           釘 ding, nail, on 丁 ding, a male child. Women touched them
           for sons. THIS IS THE THEME. See DECISIONS 30.

  度厄     crossing over misfortune. The bridge-crossing framed as
           passing out of one condition into another.

Why this and not Ghost Month: it is a WALK with a RULE SET and a
YEAR-AHEAD OUTCOME, which is structurally identical to arsgang. Ghost
Month is a season, not a journey.

RESEARCH TODO: find the actual Ming/Qing sources. 帝京景物略 (Beijing,
1635) is the one I am most confident carries a 走百病 passage. Local
gazetteers 地方志 for Jiangnan variants. Get the wording; the almanac
app should quote it.


=============================================================
THE THREE FIRES
=============================================================

三把火  san ba huo   [B]

A living person carries three flames - one on each shoulder, one on the
crown of the head - visible to spirits, not to people. While they burn,
the dead cannot easily see or touch you. Associated with 陽氣, yang
vitality.

Ways they are said to go out:
  - someone taps you on the shoulder at night              [B]
  - you turn your head sharply to look behind you          [B]
  - you answer aloud when something calls your name        [B]
  - illness, exhaustion, grief                             [B]

This belief travels with Ghost Month customs but is not confined to
them, so using it inside a first-lunar-month frame is defensible. Flag
it in the almanac as widely held rather than textually fixed.

Design note: the "do not turn to look behind you" rule appears in BOTH
this belief and in 走百病 accounts. That overlap is the seam the whole
game is built on. Verify it holds up; if it does, lead the press with it.


=============================================================
DIVINATION
=============================================================

擲筊  jiaobei / bwa bwei   [A]
Two crescent blocks, one flat face and one curved face each. Thrown
before a deity to ask a yes/no question.
   one flat up, one curved up   聖筊  approval, yes
   both flat faces up           笑筊  the "laughing" cast - unclear,
                                      the question was wrong, ask again
   both curved faces up         陰筊  no
Convention in many temples: ask three times for a serious matter.
Overwhelmingly Fujian/Taiwan-associated today but present across the
south historically. VERIFY it is defensible in late-Qing Jiangnan before
making it the core verb. If it is not, it moves to a Fujianese character
who brought it with her, per DECISIONS 11.

求籤  qiu qian / kau chim   [A]
Shaking a cylinder until one numbered bamboo stick falls out. The number
maps to a printed poem, usually four lines, graded auspicious to
inauspicious. The poem is oblique and requires interpretation, often by
a temple attendant. Standard practice: confirm the stick with 擲筊.
We do not provide the attendant.

扶乩  fuji, planchette / spirit-writing   [A]
A suspended stylus, held by two people, writes characters in sand or
ash, dictated by a spirit. Enormously popular in the Qing among literati.
Real, textual, and visually perfect for us. See DECISIONS 51.

黃曆 / 通書  the almanac   [A]
Per-day structure, all standard:
   宜   auspicious for  (marry, travel, break ground, bury)
   忌   avoid
   沖   which zodiac sign the day clashes with, and its direction
   值神  the presiding spirit of the day
   吉時  the auspicious hours
This is the companion app. It is a real document format and it is
already eerie. Do not embellish it.


=============================================================
THE BESTIARY
=============================================================

水鬼  shui gui, the drowned   [A]
Someone who drowned and cannot leave the water or be reborn until they
pull in a substitute - 找替身, finding a replacement. Deeply attested,
and specifically strong in canal and river country, which is why the
setting is a water town. Its whole existence is one obligation aimed at
the player. Some accounts: a shui gui that refuses to take a substitute
is eventually promoted to a local water god. That mercy is a puzzle.

姑獲鳥  gu huo niao, the night-bird woman   [A]
Women dead in childbirth become a bird that flies at night and takes
or marks other people's children. Also 夜行游女, 天帝少女, and tangled
with 九頭鳥 in some regions. Recorded from the Six Dynasties onward -
玄中記 is the citation I would chase first. Said to leave a mark on
clothing left out overnight, which is why you bring the washing in
after dark. This is the Myling seat and the thematic core.

畫皮  hua pi, the painted skin   [A as literature]
A demon that wears a human hide it paints itself. From 聊齋志異
(Pu Songling, c. 1740) - so it is LITERARY, not folk-anonymous, and the
almanac must say so. Attributing a Pu Songling story to "ancient Chinese
folklore" is exactly the sloppiness that gets noticed.

石敢當  shi gandang   [A]
An inscribed stone set at a road's end, a T-junction, or a bridgehead to
block malign influence.泰山石敢當 is the common full form. Real,
physical, still standing in villages today. A thing that refuses passage
is a locked door with three thousand years of documentation.

五通神  wu tong shen   [A]
Southern local gods, Jiangnan especially, of ambiguous or frankly
predatory character - associated with sudden wealth and with sexual
menace toward women. Officially suppressed in the Qing, which is itself
a story beat. The god you bargain with who is not good.

買路錢  mai lu qian, road-buying money   [B]
Paper money scattered for the dead to clear a funeral route. Picking up
money found on the road at night takes on what it was paying for.
A prohibition with a visible object attached - ideal.

叫魂  jiao hun, soul-calling   [A]
Calling a startled soul back, usually a child's, by name, at a doorway
or crossroads. Philip Kuhn's SOULSTEALERS documents the 1768 panic
around it. Relevant to DECISIONS 19 and 52: the thing behind you calling
your name may not be hostile. It may be someone calling you home.
